# Kaldvik

A northern fjord in early winter, at about 64 degrees north. The sun stands twelve degrees up at noon and is down by four; the rest is blue dusk, a long night, and on most nights the northern lights.

The land is a plateau of basalt, flow laid on flow until it stood near two hundred metres over the sea. Before the ice a river drained the plateau west, and its valleys set the lines the ice would follow. Through the ice ages a great glacier gathered in the upper valley east of the head, took in a second from the south-east where the two valleys meet, and went west to the sea in two long bends, grinding a trough far below sea level. Its smaller tributaries could not cut as deep: when the ice went, the north one (Hengdal) was left hanging high on the fjord wall, and others round the plateau the same. The last of the ice still sits in the cirque at the top of Hengdal, Kaldbreen glacier. Where the great glacier stopped it dropped a moraine across the mouth, and the sea only just covers the sill.

The walls step up in lava-flow ledges that hold the snow, so from the water they are dark rock ruled in white. Spruce crowd the lower slopes and the head of the fjord, where the river's gravel flat is the only level ground: that is where the hamlet is.

Inspired by Iceland's eastern fjords, western Norway and the Christmas markets of the north.

Up on the fell the wind keeps the rises blown bare to the black rock; knobs of basalt stand through the snow, the ground is cracked into frost polygons, and the snow lies in hard ridges across the wind. On the north-west fell the Trollkonur, old volcanic necks, stand thirty metres high; three sea stacks, the Drangar, stand off the mouth. Above the head a hot pool, Laugin, steams in any frost, and on the south fell a dry field of fumaroles does the same.

In December the stream from Kaldbreen goes over the lip of Hengdal and hangs down the wall as ice, Hengdalsfossen, with a cone of ice at its foot. Isvika, the corner where the south-east valley comes down to the water, freezes first, then the north cove and the shallows at the head; the open water between steams with sea smoke on the coldest days, and on clear ones the air glitters with diamond dust.

The hamlet is a dozen buildings on the slope above the head: Falu-red rorbu on stilts at the water, a stone boathouse, stockfish drying on the racks, red, white and ochre houses, an Icelandic turf house with two white gables, and the white church up the hill. Through December the square between them is a Christmas market: striped stalls hung with lights, a straw Yule goat, and a tall spruce with a star on it. The lanterns and windows light at dusk, which here is mid-afternoon, and smoke rises from every chimney.

Beyond the hamlet there is more to walk to. A track runs up the valley to the saeter, the summer farm, shut since September with its fold empty. A path climbs the ledges of the north wall to Laugin, where a lantern is left burning so nobody walks into the pool in the dark. Out on the south point of the middle fjord is Nesvaer, the outer station: two rorbu and a landing stage, used in the herring season and closed for the winter. On the headland north of the mouth stands Ytternes light, which the fleet paid for after the snow of 1888, when two boats missed the mouth; both went onto the Drangar. North of the light the north-west wind drives the sea ice onto the open coast, and it stays fast to the cliffs until April: the pack. The Nordstjerne, an expedition ship bound for the ice, was caught in it in the autumn of 1915 and never let go. Her crew walked to the hamlet in the spring. She stands in it still every winter, masts leaning, sails frozen on the yards. Inside the fjord, on the south shore, is Svartsand, the black strand, where a tongue of old lava stands in columns. A woodcutter lives in Skogen, the south-west wood, and his smoke can be seen from the water. The stream out of that wood ends above the outer fjord as a second frozen fall, Skardfossen. Three cairns, the varder, stand on Kaldhovd, Vesthovd and Sudhovd; they are older than the hamlet, and from each you can see the next. In Hengdal, below the glacier, is the depot the Nordstjerne's crew laid in the autumn of 1915 for a journey onto the ice that was never made.

Orcas follow the herring in with the humpbacks, one in seven of them white; eiders raft off the ice and seals haul out on it; sea eagles quarter the fjord, ravens work the racks, and on the fell there are ptarmigan in winter white and an arctic fox. On still days with a south wind the north wall gives a glider all the lift it needs.
