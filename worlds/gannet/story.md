# Gannet Harbor

A whaling town on a grey bay north of the titan's beach, swept and painted and
rigged for sea, with nobody in it. Behind the town walled sheep pasture rolls up
to the forest; north of the fields a cold river comes down out of the redwoods;
offshore two granite islands stand in the mouth of the bay, one with a research
station on it that was left in a week, the other with a sea cave under it.

## The land

The coast here is the same coast as Ribshore's, a day's walk south: low granite
country lifted a little out of the sea, rising east into Sitka spruce. Where
Ribshore runs straight, this coast is let in into a broad bight, Mercy Bay, six
hundred metres deep and shallow all through, with a sand-and-eelgrass floor, clam
flats along its edges and salt marsh in its calm south-east corner. Two blocks of
harder rock stand in its mouth: Hake Island, the larger, cliffed on its seaward
side where the swell comes in and low on the side that faces the town, and
Hogback Island to the north, a narrow whaleback, higher and steeper. Bull kelp
grows on the rock between them. A few granite stacks stand off the islands, the
last of older headlands.

South of the bay the beach and dunes carry on from Ribshore's and turn in under
the forest. North of the bay the Cold River comes down from the redwood country
beyond the map, joined by a branch off the hills in the north-east corner, and
runs west over rounded rock to its mouth at the end of the bay's north shore.
Coast redwood grows along the whole northern edge and down to the shore in the
north-west, the old groves among them never cut. In the far north-east corner the
redwoods turn into something else: the edge of the faerie wood that lies north
of here.

## The inland whale

Ribshore's giants hunted the great baleen whales. One of their kills came ashore
here when the sea stood higher and the beach was further in, and lies now
across the line where the dunes meet the forest: a baleen whale eighty metres
long, its jaw upturned, its ribs standing in the dune grass on one side and
among the spruce on the other. Sand has drifted into the half that lies in the
dunes. On the forest side the vertebrae are under moss and fern, and spruce
grow up between the ribs.

## The town

Fishermen from the south wintered at the bay in the 1680s, and there was a
meetinghouse by 1702. The town grew on whaling. From the 1790s its ships went
round the Horn on voyages of three years, and the captains built their houses
up the hill with walks on the roofs to watch for them. In 1841 a Gannet Harbor
company set up a shore station at Try-works Creek, on Ribshore, and ran it for
six seasons; they wrote that they would not go under the ribs at night. The
lighthouse on Gannet Head, south of the creek, was kept by town men from 1894.

The town is on the bay's east shore: streets running up the slope from the
water, white and red clapboard houses, a church, a customs house, an inn, an
almshouse row, a graveyard. Timber wharves run out on piles from the
waterfront. Two whalers lie at them, rigged and ready for sea, a third rides at
anchor out in the bay, and a fourth is hauled out on the marine railway at the
south end, careened and half re-coppered. A small light stands on the point
beyond it. Behind the town the fields go up the slope inside dry-stone walls
laid from the stones the ploughs turned up, with farmsteads and sheds among
them, an orchard or two, and a sugar shack at the forest edge. A lane runs
north over the fields, fords the Cold River and ends in the redwoods; a branch
goes west to Shad Landing, a hamlet of net sheds and cottages at the river
mouth with a ferry stage. The Post Road leaves the town to the east.

All of it is kept. The paint is sound, the lawns are cut, the ships' rigging is
set up taut, and there is nobody in any of it. Nobody says when they went.

## The Thankful

In the fog of 1849 the whaler *Thankful* came back past Hake Island with a full
hold, was seen once off Hogback, and was never seen again. The town said she
sailed into the island.

She did, after a fashion. On Hogback's south side, eight metres down in the
kelp, a crack the swell worked open runs north into the rock. Swim it and it
rises into a chamber under the middle of the island where the sea comes in at
its foot and a slope of sand climbs to a dry back, lit by two chimneys up to
the turf. The *Thankful* lies there half buried in the sand, her masts broken
against the roof.

## Hake Island Marine Station

In 1952 the navy built a listening post on Hake Island's low east side, with a
dish on the roof of its main building and a radio shack. In 1961 a university
took it over as a marine station and studied the kelp, the bones on Ribshore and
the puffins that nest in burrows in the turf on the island's west cliff tops; a
banding hut stands near the colony with its records in it. In 1979 the station
was left in a week. The notebooks are still on the desks, and the landing stage
has half fallen in.

The puffins are still there, standing about in rows at the cliff edge, with
gulls patrolling for eggs and cormorants on the stacks below.

## What is not explained

- Where the people of Gannet Harbor went, and who keeps the town.
- The station's last tapes, still in the radio shack, are a hydrophone recording
  of a slow regular pulse that keeps the same time as the titan's blowhole on
  Ribshore. The dish on Hake points south.

The weather is Ribshore's: grey, fog off the sea most mornings, the islands
coming and going in it.
