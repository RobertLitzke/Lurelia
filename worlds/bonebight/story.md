# Bonebight

East of Ribshore and south of the Kitehold Plain the coast turns. The spruce
hills that stand behind Ribshore's dunes come round to the south-east and
meet a wide grey bay, and between the forest and the water lies a belt of
dunes a kilometre long. A ribcage stands in the middle of them, taller than
anything else on this coast, and a town is built inside it.

## The land

The bay is the mouth of the Lessa. The river comes down off the Kitehold
farms, slows into reed marsh and pools along the north edge, and loses itself
in the bay through a dozen channels. It has been bringing sand down for as
long as the coast has had its shape, and the sea has thrown that sand back up
the shore, so the coast has grown out eastward into the bay. What was the
beach when the titans died is now three or four hundred metres inland, under
the dunes.

The dunes are the same as Ribshore's: ridges a few metres high, combed by the
north-west wind, held down by dunegrass, lupine and sea buckthorn and broken
open in blowouts. They end in the north against the last of the Plain, where
Kitehold's tan bunchgrass thins out into sand.

Behind the dunes, in the west, the ground rises into Sitka spruce, the same
forest as Ribshore's. In its north-west corner the spruce closes round a small
lake in a hollow, Kagami Pond. Its outlet, Mirror Creek, runs south down the
foot of the hills between the forest and the dunes. Below Bonebight it splits:
the larger arm turns west through the hills and becomes the creek that cuts
across Ribshore's dunes to the sea, and the smaller arm braids south through
the dunes to the coast at Vila Azul.

The bay is deeper than any water on this coast, thirty metres and more off its
southern half. Its floor is sand and silt from the river over the old basalt.
Out in the middle of it stands Frondwell, the drowned rim of an old volcano:
a ring of basalt four hundred metres across, broken open on the south-west.
Its crest is a metre or two under the sea for most of its length, and here and
there it comes up as an islet carrying a few shore pines and spruce. Inside is
a sheltered lagoon fifteen metres deep. Outside, the walls drop to the bay
floor. The cold water off the ring grows giant kelp in a forest round the
whole rim, the stipes rising from the rock to the surface and lying out along
it in a canopy.

## The giants

The titans that died on Ribshore died here too. The largest of all came
ashore on what was then the beach and died on its belly. The sea took its
skull, its spine and its tail long ago and broke them up; pieces of vertebrae
and teeth lie scattered through the dunes where the storms dropped them. Its
ribs were too big to move. Sixteen pairs still stand, rooted in the old beach
under the sand, bowing out and curling back in over a lane fifty metres wide
and never meeting overhead. The tallest are near two hundred metres, larger
than Ribshore's. The dunes have blown away from round their roots, so the
ribcage stands in a long hollow a few metres below the dune tops.

North of it, where the dunes give way to the Plain, lies a smaller one, five
hundred metres long. It came ashore later, when the coast had already built
out, and lies with its skull in the shore dunes facing the bay and its spine
running back north-west into the grass, its tail curled in the Plain.

## Bonebight

People came to the bay in the 1790s for the bone. Titan bone is dense and
does not rot, and it carves like ivory. The marrow inside the oldest bones has
turned to a pale crystal that hums very faintly when it is warm, and the
crystal sold well. The first bone-cutters camped inside the ribcage for the
shelter it gave from the wind, and the camp became a town: cedar-shingled
houses and workshops along the lane between the rib rows, bone yards where
the cut blocks are stacked, and boat sheds where they build boats out of the
driftwood the river brings down. The town has long since outgrown the
lane: it fills the ground under the ribs and between them and spills out
past both ends of the cage and down the road east to the harbour, a small
cove in the shore bank they call the bight. Its quay is a wall of cut bone
blocks, with a timber pier, a fish house and a net shed on piles, boat sheds
on the bank above, a breakwater of bone heaped out from the north point,
and the town's boats tied up or at anchor.

Under the ribs, on their damp shaded undersides, grow glass-ears: shelf
fungi a metre across in tiers, amber and teal, that glow at night. They
grow on nothing but titan bone, and the light from them is the town's night
light.

Off the bight and in the lee of the point at Vila Azul the bay is farmed:
oyster racks in timber pens, and alongside them pens of drift-bells. These
are jellies the size of a beach ball with ribbed domes. At low water they
rest in the pens; when the tide rises they fill a chamber with gas and lift
a few feet into the air on their mooring lines, and bob there until it
falls.

North of the town the old pilgrim road comes down from Kitehold. It is worn
three metres down into the Plain the whole way, and it keeps on down into the
sand of the dunes until the sand fills it in.

## The delta

The Lessa's mouth is a marsh of reed and sedge with channels between. Where
the marsh meets the bay the storms pile the driftwood: whole spruce and cedar
trunks two and three metres thick, silver, heaped into walls along the shore
that you weave through on foot. The easternmost channels are clear water over
cobbles, knee-deep and wide, and in autumn the salmon run up them to spawn,
crimson and green against the dark stones, and black bears come down out of
the hills to the flat banks to fish them.

## Vila Azul

In the 1860s Azorean whalers came round with the Gannet Harbor ships, and when
the whaling ended a few families stayed on at the south end of the dunes,
where Mirror Creek reaches the sea. They fished, and they found good clay in
the creek banks and built kilns. Vila Azul is their town: white walls covered
in glazed tile, cobalt and turquoise, saffron and terracotta, round plazas
paved in the same tile. The families built towers, each trying to outdo the
last, until the skyline was a dozen of them, stepped and crenellated. Striped
canopies shade the streets, and on a windy day a kite or two flies from a
tower top.

## Pelagos Outpost

In 1971 an oceanographic foundation built a research outpost on Frondwell to
study what the titans left behind: the glass-ears, the drift-bells, the
crystal, and the kelp forest round the ring, which grows larger than kelp
grows anywhere else. It is concrete modules on legs in the 1970s manner,
painted in harvest gold, burnt orange and avocado, with orange geodesic dive
domes, radar rooms, and portholes below the waterline looking out into the
kelp. The stations are tall steel capsules standing straight up out of the
sea, faded orange, maroon and avocado and streaked with rust, weed and
barnacles thick round their feet: the base station south of the ring, and
three more on the southern rim, all four joined by truss walkways on legs.
A fifth, Station Calypso, stands alone on the north-east rim. A submersible
is still tied up at the base.

The outpost put in the chain ferry from Vila Azul: a cedar-roofed skiff with
tile inlays, kept up by green glass floats, that runs out to the base station
and back on a chain along the bay floor. It has no engine. A slab of raw
marrow-crystal is bolted to its deck, and the skiff goes back and forth on
it, humming, and has done every day since 1974.

The outpost was given up in 1984.

## What is not explained

In 1977 the divers at Station Calypso found a piece of black glass in the
lagoon floor, the size of a car, warm to the touch, of the same kind as the
skystone that fell on Glassfall. The kelp round it had gone pale. By 1984
the pale had spread up the station's legs and over its decks, and the station
was abandoned to it. It is still spreading: the coral-like glass growth and
pale weed reach out along the north-east rim into the kelp, with spiral pines
on the islet nearest, and the further from Calypso, the more ordinary
everything is again.

Nobody knows what the crystal on the ferry runs on.

In the north-west corner of the spruce, out of sight of everything else,
stands a temple on Kagami Pond, in a dense wood of coast redwood and spruce,: a cedar hall with open wooden floors, a gate
standing in the water, stone lanterns and a raked gravel garden. No road goes
there, only a path that fades out in the trees. It is swept, and the lanterns
are lit at dusk. Neither Bonebight nor Vila Azul says who keeps it.
