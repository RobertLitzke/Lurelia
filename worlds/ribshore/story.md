# Ribshore

A grey northern coast of wide wet sand and long grassy dunes, with the bones of
a giant lying across it. Ribshore is a mile and a half of shore, a basalt
headland at its north end, a strip of dark spruce behind the dunes, and the
skeleton of an animal fourteen hundred metres long lying along the beach, its
skull in the surf and its tail in the dune grass.

## The giants

Before the coast had its present shape the sea held animals the size of
islands. They were toothed hunters, long-jawed, built like the whales that came
after them and a hundred times their size, and they hunted the great baleen
whales the way an orca hunts a seal. Nobody saw them. What is known is in the
bones, and the bones say three things: that the giants lived, that they
hunted, and that they died out, all at once or nearly, long enough ago that
their skeletons are stone-hard and lichened and nothing on this coast
remembers them.

The largest came ashore here. It lies on its belly along the beach, head to
the south in the surf and tail to the north, where the spine leaves the beach
and curls up into the dunes. The sea took the flesh and the wind took the sand
off the bones and put it back again, many times. The bone has gone dark grey.
What stands now:

- **The skull**, at the south end, settled upright at the waterline with the
  surf running round it. Its jaws stand open: the lower jaw on the sand, the
  long upper jaw lifted, and both still set with teeth taller than a house.
  Inside the skull is a room you can walk into from behind, lit by the eye
  sockets and the blowhole. A few teeth that fell out stand in the wet sand
  north of it like stones; more lie on the sea floor off the head.
- **The ribs**, sixteen pairs standing up out of the beach like tusks, bowing
  out and then in over the spine and never meeting, the tallest near two
  hundred metres. No two are alike. Many are broken off in the air, and the
  broken pieces lie beside them.
- **The spine**, fifty vertebrae running north from the ribs along the beach
  and up into the dunes, each one worn flat on top and half buried. Sand has
  drifted level into the gaps between them, so the spine is a road of bone:
  you can walk it, or ride it, from the dune grass down to the skull. Its
  side processes lie out into the sand like groynes, and here and there a
  neural spine still stands, leaning back toward the tail.
- **The flipper and the shoulder blade**, fallen from the body onto the
  landward side of the ribs: a hand of bones as long as a street, and a dish
  of bone that holds the rain.

Its prey lies at the north end of the beach under the headland: a baleen whale
eighty metres long, its jaw upturned in the sand so the plates of its baleen
stand up in two dark rows. And in the dunes near the titan's tail lies a calf
of its own kind, fifty metres long, curled the way it died.

## The coast since

The coast is ordinary, and old. The prevailing wind comes off the sea from the
north-west and has piled the sand into dunes a few metres high and forty or
fifty apart, ridge after ridge for half a kilometre inland, held down by
dunegrass, lupine and thickets of sea buckthorn, bare where the wind breaks
through. At the dunes' foot is the wrack line, whole spruce trunks thrown up by
winter storms and bleached silver. Behind the dunes the ground rises into Sitka
spruce. A creek comes down out of the forest and cuts across the dunes to the
sea.

At the north end a block of basalt stands out into the sea as a headland, with
cliffs, a cap of wind-cut turf and shore pine, and shelves at the waterline
cut into pools: mussels, sea stars, green anemones, sea palms where the surf
hits. Bull kelp grows on the reefs off it. A few sea stacks stand off the
shore, the last of older headlands.

South of the creek, the stumps of a spruce forest stand in the wet sand. The
land here went down a few metres in an earthquake three hundred years ago and
the sea killed the trees where they stood.

Elk come down out of the forest to graze the dune hollows at dusk. Walruses
haul out under the ribs, which give them shade and shelter from the wind.
Albatrosses ride the air that the wind throws up over the ribcage. Crabs
bigger than any crab ought to be pick over the beach and the shallows. Under
the water the bones that fell off the head into deeper water are still being eaten: mats
of bacteria on the silt, white anemones on everything that stands, octopus in
the sockets and sixgill sharks slow in the dark water.

## People

People came late, and few. In the 1840s whalers ran a station at the creek
mouth for six seasons, boiling down the whales they took offshore in a
try-works on the shore. The brick hearth and its two iron pots are still there.
They wrote that they would not go out under the ribs at night.

The lighthouse on the headland was built in 1894 and kept for sixty years. It
is empty now.

## What is not explained

- A harpoon twenty metres long, rusted through, is stuck in one of the
  vertebrae near the hips. It was there before the whalers came. Nobody made
  it.
- The blowhole spouts on a rhythm, whatever the sea is doing.

The place is quiet. The weather is mostly grey, the fog comes in off the sea
most mornings and some days does not lift, and the bones stand in it.
