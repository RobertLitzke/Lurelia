# Thornpan

An inland delta in a dry country. A river arrives out of the north-east, runs
down a trough between two rock walls, and then stops being a river: it spreads
into a fan of clear shallow channels over white sand, and what the fan does not
drink ends in salt pans in the south-west. Nothing drains out of this country.
Everything that comes in stays and dries.

The land round the water is golden grass on rolling sand hills, with granite
pushing through in stacks of boulders. The people are fishers and cattle
keepers in thatched villages, and they measure the year by a flood that comes
when the sky is cleanest.

## The rock and the water that left

The floor of the whole country is granite, and in the low parts it is buried
under its own sand hundreds of metres deep. Where it stands up it stands up in
domes with loose boulders stacked on them, split off along the curved shells
the rock weathers in. Seven of these hills break the grass: the Stacks. The
biggest, Lamp Kopje, stands twenty-four metres over the plain and can be seen
from everywhere in the country that is not behind another one.

Two faults run north-east to south-west, forty metres apart in height and
about a hundred and sixty metres apart on the ground. The block between them
dropped. The river found the trough and has used it ever since, which is why
the upper river is straight and narrow and the lower one is not.

Before any of this mattered, the basin was a lake. It filled to within a metre
of thirty-four, drowned everything but the tops of the Stacks, and held at that
level long enough to leave a mark: a band of grey-white crust, a metre thick,
on every rock face in the country that reaches that height, which is most of
the low country and the flanks of every one of the Stacks. Close up, the band
is shells. Barnacles, cemented in plates, with the look of something that grew
in moving salt water and died where it grew. There is no sea within a month's
walking.

The lake went. Nobody here knows why, and the country keeps three
inconveniently clear records of it:

The **White Pans** in the south-west are its floor, swept bare, cracked into
plates, bleached to the point where on a bright afternoon you cannot look at
them. They are the quietest ground in the world. Nothing grows on them, nothing
crosses them, and shells lie on them in drifts.

The **wrecks**. Sea-going hulls, four of them, with the proportions of ships
built for weather no lake has: one careened on the pan with her masts still up,
one broken in the reeds of the Spread with her ribs out of the grass, two on
their sides in the flood meadows where the cattle rub on them. Their timbers
are barnacled to the rail. Sedge's council house is built of planks off the
nearest one and nobody finds that odd.

The **Lamp**, on the top of Lamp Kopje, which stands twenty-six metres over
the grass: a stone tower, round, ten metres of it still standing, built to carry a light. It faces south-west over the pans,
which is to say over the deepest part of a lake that has not existed for
fourteen thousand years.

## The flood

Rain that falls somewhere upstream of everything these people know of arrives
here five months later, in the dry season, when the sky has not had a cloud in
it for weeks. The Thorn comes down the Narrows brown and heavy, spills out of
its banks at the head of the Spread, and pushes out through the fan a few
hundred metres a day, running clear by the time it is past the first islands
because the sand takes everything out of it. For two months the grassland is a
sheet of ankle-deep water you can see your feet through, and then it goes back.

Everything in the country is arranged around that. The channels are cut by
animals, not water: the elephants keep them open, and when the elephants stop
using one it closes in a season and the water takes another way. The villages
stand on sand knolls a metre and a half up. Each has a flood post at the
landing with the years' heights cut into it, the deep ones deeper, and the two
that overtopped the post marked on the house behind it.

## The trees

Baobabs. On the dry ground they stand alone, a hundred metres apart, grey and
smooth and far too thick for their height, bare half the year with their
branches up like roots. Some are enormous and some of the enormous ones are
hollow, and a hollow baobab is not a tree here, it is a building:

- **The Cistern Tree**, on the dry bank above Longwater, holds water. Its
  hollow has been smoothed and sealed at the bottom and it is filled by hand in
  the flood against the end of the dry. There is a ladder of pegs and a bucket.
- **The Bee Tree**, on the grass south of the Spread, has pegs hammered up the
  outside to a colony thirteen metres up. The ground round it is bare from
  smoke fires. Nobody goes near it at midday.
- **The Tomb Tree**, east of the pans, is closed with a slab. Two generations
  of one family are in it. It is the oldest thing in the country that anyone
  here will claim.
- **The Council Tree**, in Sedge, is open at the bottom on two sides and used
  as a room. Benches, a hearth scar, the ship's planks.

Away from the baobabs: acacias in flat-topped stands on the hills, mopane
scrub where the sand is thinnest, fan palms in rings on the islands of the
Spread (one palm nut goes through an elephant and comes out on an island, so
the rings are elephant-planted and the people know it), and dead baobabs on the
pan rim, standing grey and holding nothing.

The upper river is the one green place. Along the north-west bank, where the
scarp shades the water and the wet ground never dries, there is closed forest:
figs rooted into the rock, high crowns shut over the channel, vines, ferns,
no view of anything. The south-east bank, forty metres away across the water,
is open meadow running back to the grass hills, and from it the forest reads as
a green wall with birds going in and out of it. The two banks are the same
river and have nothing else in common.

## The people

Three places, perhaps two hundred people between them.

**Sedge** sits on the biggest island in the Spread: nine round houses of daub
under conical thatch, a council tree, racks of drying fish, a stock pen for the
dry season and a landing with five mokoro drawn up on the sand. The paths
between the houses are bare, and they go to the water.

**Longwater** is upstream on the open bank, where the cattle are: round houses
in two groups, granaries up on posts, a thorn kraal, the Cistern Tree on the
rise above.

**Barnacle Camp** is a fishing camp on a sand bank in the lower Spread, three
shelters and a net store, built beside the hull of the broken ship and partly
out of it.

They fish, keep cattle on the dry ground, grow nothing to speak of, and hunt
very little because there is no need. They do not write. Names, flood heights,
and the marks on the posts are the whole record.

**The old people** are a separate matter. Somebody built the Ringstone — a
dry-stone enclosure, walls two metres and no mortar, laid in courses with the
stones fitted, inside the summit boulders of Lamp Kopje — and painted the
shelter in the kopje below it in red ochre: cattle, a hand, and a row of ships.
They were gone before the present people came and the present people keep off
the summit. The Lamp is older than the Ringstone and was there to be built
against.

## The animals

Large and everywhere and not much afraid, which is the first thing anyone would
notice. Elephants in families crossing the channels belly-deep and shoulder
deep, lechwe in herds of thirty in the flood meadows that run splashing when
you come on them, wildebeest and zebra on the dry grass, buffalo in the reeds,
giraffe on the acacia hills, warthog everywhere, hippo in the deeper pools with
their backs out, crocodiles in the channels, and lions asleep on the warm
granite of the Stacks with vultures in the air over whatever they left.

The birds are the reason to be on the water at dawn. Carmine bee-eaters nest in
the cut outside bank of the second bend below the Narrows — a face of earth
with a thousand holes in it and the air in front of it full — and they hunt out
over the water all morning and follow any grass fire in the country for the
insects it drives up. Fish eagles, herons, egrets, hornbills in the mopane.

## What is not explained

Three things, and they are all the same thing.

The barnacle band on the rock, twelve metres over the highest the flood has
ever reached.

The ships, and the row of them painted in the shelter by people who had never
seen a sea.

And the pans at the hour before sunset, when there is open water on them from a
distance, with trees standing in it and sometimes a hull, which is gone by the
time you are close enough to see it properly. The people call it the lake
coming back. They are not troubled by it and they do not go out on to the pans
in the evening.
