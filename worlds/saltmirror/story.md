# Saltmirror

A salt pan so flat that a shower turns it into a mirror. Eleven radio dishes,
each a hundred metres across, stand on it in a broken Y, paint gone chalky grey, all of them still turned up
to the sky the way they were parked the last night anyone worked them. In the
north-east corner white gypsum dunes run up against a grey range covered in
fynbos. Nobody has lived here for ten years.

## The pan

The floor of the basin is the bed of a lake. In the cold ages it filled to the
foot of the range and stood there long enough to cut two shorelines into the
mountain, one about twelve metres over the pan and one about twenty-six; they
run round every spur in the north-east as benches, easy to see in the low
light. When the lake went it left its salt, and the salt is still there:
a hard crust a hand thick over grey mud, drawn into plates by the sun.

The lowest part of the pan, south of the middle, never quite dries. Brine
stands there a few centimetres deep over a slick of mud, and the flamingos
come for the brine shrimp in it. In the north-west there is other water, fed from below: the Turquoise, a
lake a metre or two deep so clear and blue-green it looks lit from
underneath, with brine eyes round it that go down further than you can
see, each ringed with orange and olive mats. Three knolls of the range's
quartzite stand out of the salt, the lake's islands once, and the cacti
crowd them: cardon, saguaro and ocotillo on the only dry rock on the pan.

The rest of the pan floods only when a
shower crosses it, which is two or three times a year. Then the water lies
an inch deep for a day or two from one edge to the other, and the ground
and the sky become the same thing. That is where the name comes from. The
array's staff called it the Mirror before anyone wrote it on a map.

## The dunes and the range

The wind here blows from the south-west most of the year. It takes the
gypsum the lake left behind, grain by grain, and drives it north-east across
the pan until the range stops it. So the dunes are piled against the
mountain foot, white as the salt and soft underfoot, with soaptree yuccas on
the crests and pale lizards that match the sand.

The range itself is quartzite, grey and hard, standing about a hundred metres
over the pan at its highest. It catches what rain there is, and it is covered
in fynbos: heath, reeds and proteas in a low grey-green fleece over the rock,
with silver trees in the ravines. The ravines run only after a storm. Where
they come out onto the flat they have spread fans of grey gravel, and the
fans are where the cacti grow: columns of cardón and saguaro, ocotillo, cholla
and agave, with tortoises and rattlesnakes among them. Lizards run between
the bushes all day.

Dry storms come in over the range at dusk in late summer. They bring no rain,
only lightning, and after a shower has left the pan wet the flash shows twice:
once in the cloud and once in the ground under your feet.

## The array

In 1959 an astronomer from the coast named Ilse Varga drove out here looking
for a quiet place. Radio telescopes need silence more than they need dark:
no towns, no roads, no transmitters, and ground flat enough to lay out a
long array on. She found all of it. Her first instrument was an optical dome
on the western summit of the range, which still stands, its shutter rusted
half open.

The Saltmirror Array was built from 1964 to 1971. Twelve concrete pads were
poured along three dirt roads that leave a hub in the middle of the pan, the
pads further apart the further out they go, as an interferometer's are. The
roads are not straight. The surveyors followed the firmest ground, and the
crust is not firm everywhere. The north arm is the longest; the south-west
arm was cut short when the money ran low, and its last pad was poured but
never got a dish. Eleven dishes came in pieces by road, each a hundred metres across,
the largest ever built, assembled at the hub and carried out to its pad
by the transporter, a crawler on two tracks each as wide as a road.

Most of the staff lived at the camp, on the gravel fan at the foot of the
range: a row of cabins, a mess hall with a mural of the solar system along
one wall, a water tower, a generator shed and a motor pool full of pickup
trucks. The control building stood at the hub, low and long with a flat
roof, louvres down the sun side and a correlator hall full of racks. In the
mid-seventies it was a good place to work. Visitors came out for open days
and were shown the dishes all turning together, which is a thing worth
seeing.

The array closed ten years ago. The official reason was money, and the
money had been going for some time. What the staff remember is the night
three weeks before the closing order: a light came down over the range from
the south-west, low and slow, with no sound, and set the fynbos burning in
a high hollow behind the eastern ridge. The night log records a transient
on every baseline at once and stops at 02:14. In the morning two people
drove the fire road up toward the smoke. Their pickup is still at the end of
the road. Nobody wrote down what they found.

When the closing order came, people left fast. The cups are still on the
mess hall tables. The pickups are where they were parked, settling onto their
rims. The dishes are still parked facing the zenith, except the one on the
east arm that was being moved that night and is still sitting on the
transporter, halfway to its pad.

## What is up in the hollow

Behind the eastern ridge, in a hollow you cannot see from the pan or from
the fire road, there is a burned scar on the mountainside, and in it a long
scorched hull lying open along one side, broken into pieces down the slope.
Its skin is not like anything else here. Small pieces of it lie in the
fynbos for a long way downhill, light, dark and faintly warm. The fynbos has
come back stronger on the burn than anywhere else on the range: fynbos needs
fire, and ten years on the proteas are thickest round the wreck.

## The twelfth pad

The empty pad is at the end of the south-west arm, out on the open salt.
At midday, when the heat shimmer stands on the pan, people who look toward
it from a long way off see a dish standing on it, white, parked facing the
sky like the others. It is gone when they get close. The staff saw it for
years before the array closed and treated it as a mirage, which is what it
looks like. Nobody has explained why the mirage is always of a dish.

## Who is here now

Nobody. The salt tortoises, as big as elephants, crossing the Mirror so
slowly that from a dish rim they seem not to move; nobody knows how old they
are, and the staff named them. Flamingos on the brine, pronghorn crossing the pan in the morning,
coyotes and jackrabbits on the fans, vultures overhead, ravens on the dish
rims, barn owls in the control building and bats in the receiver cabins.
