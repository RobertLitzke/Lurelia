# Redrock

A high desert where a plateau of pine forest breaks off in red cliffs, and the
country below falls away south-west in benches of sandstone and sand to a
reservoir that is slowly going dry. The land runs on past every horizon; the
reservoir is a drowned river canyon across the south and up the west, with the
same desert standing on its far side.

## The rock

The ground is a stack of old sediments, laid flat and never much disturbed.
Lowest, and only showing where the canyon cuts deepest, is a banded mudstone,
purple and grey and pale blue, the bed of a swampy river plain. Logs washed into
it turned to stone, and where it weathers out at the surface they lie in pieces
on the bare clay. Above it is a dark red sandstone that breaks in sheer walls,
then a thinner band of purple-red ledges, then a thick pale sandstone that was
once a desert of dunes: cream at the top, salmon lower down, its old dune faces
showing as long sloping lines in the cliffs. Over all of it, on the high
plateau in the north-east, lies a cap of black basalt.

Nobody can say what happened next in a sentence, but the land rose and the
water cut it. The edge of the plateau stepped back into two lines of cliffs:
the White Cliffs below the forest, which bound the high country in the
north-east, and lower down the Red Cliffs, more than a hundred feet of red wall
crossing the whole country on a long double bend from the east to the
north-west, cut back here and there by short side canyons. South and west of
the Red Cliffs the land is a broad sandy bench with mesas and buttes standing on it,
the pieces of cliff the water hasn't finished taking. A few are so worn that
only a spire is left. Castle Mesa in the north-west is an island of the Red
Cliffs themselves, and the Sisters are three buttes standing together south of
the highway. The beds are not quite level everywhere: they thicken
and thin from place to place, and in two places they bend up along a fold.
Erosion has stripped the high side of each fold, leaving a tilted ridge, a
reef: a long slope of bare rock climbing with the beds and a ledgy cliff on
the other side. The bigger one, the Comb, is in the south-east, and a smaller
one stands west of the Gathering. A few cracks have worn down through each
crest, and they are the ways up. Above the west shore the wind has heaped sand
off the old river beaches into a field of coral-pink dunes.

The Sorrel River rises in springs on the plateau, where water that sank
through the cinders comes out again at the edge of the basalt. It runs as a
creek through the pines, falls off the plateau through a gap in the White
Cliffs and has cut a canyon that winds south-west and then south across the
whole bench, about a hundred feet deep at the highway and deeper where it slots
through the Red Cliffs,
flat-floored, its walls climbing in ledges of red sandstone over banded clay.
South-east of the canyon the pale sandstone stands up in fins, thin walls of rock
left between joints that weathered out, and the weather has worn windows
through several of them. Out on the slickrock bench the Lone Arch is the
thinnest of them, its span worn through a fin that is almost gone.

Along the foot of the White Cliffs, frost and rain have cut the soft pink rock
into an amphitheatre of hoodoos: columns of rock, each with a harder cap.
Most stand in a crowd under the cliff. Out on the desert floor south of the
highway, far from any cliff that could have made them, eleven more stand in a
rough ring. Nobody has explained them.

On every cliff in the country that rises high enough, at the same height to
within a foot, there is a pale band a few feet thick, as if water once stood
there long enough to leave its mark: along the Red Cliffs, around every butte
and fin, across the face of Harlow Mesa. It is far above any water the
reservoir has held, and perfectly level. The miners called it the high-water
mark.

## Fire

A little over nine hundred years ago a small volcano opened on the plateau. It
built a black cinder cone, sent a tongue of basalt west through the pines, and
left a lava tube under the flow whose roof has fallen in at two places. Cold
air pools in the tube and the floor holds ice into summer.

## The canyon people

The farmers who lived in Sorrel Canyon then grew corn on the floor where the
river spread. They built stone granaries in an alcove high in the west wall,
reached by a talus slope and toeholds, and sealed them with little doorways.
They left about seven hundred and forty years ago, in a long drought. The
granaries are still there, in the west wall a quarter of a mile below the bridge, and potsherds and arrowheads still turn up on the
canyon floor and the benches.

## Harlow

In 1882 a prospector found silver in the hills in the south-west corner of the
country, at the foot of a long mesa. Within two years Harlow had a main street
of false-fronted buildings, a saloon, a store, an assay office, a stamp mill
on the slope, a hoisting frame over the main shaft, rows of miners' shacks and a
cemetery on the hill. The company drove a haulage tunnel through the toe of the
mesa to bring ore out to the mill. The tailings from the mill spread in a pale
yellow fan below it.

When the price of silver collapsed in 1893 the mine closed in a month and the
town emptied in a year. In the 1950s men came back looking for uranium, put up
a steel headframe and a few sheds, found little, and left again. Harlow has
been falling down for a hundred and thirty years: roofs first, then the
boardwalks and the timber, then the stone. The purple glass of old bottles
still lies in the sand around it, turned that colour by the sun.

## Bitterwater and the road

In 1880 a handful of farming families settled where the Sorrel came out onto
the flats near the big river, and called the place Bitterwater. They built a
stone chapel, dug ditches and planted orchards.

In 1929 the state built a steel arch bridge across Sorrel Canyon, and the next
year a highway along the foot of the Red Cliffs, where the ground is flattest,
from the river crossing in the west to the ferry in the south-east. In 1954 the
Pruitt family built a filling station beside it, about a third of the way down
from the plateau: two pumps under a canopy, a store, a water tank, a sign on a
tall pole.

## The reservoir

In 1963 a dam closed on the big river downstream, and over the next fifteen
years Lake Corwin backed up into every low canyon. The families of Bitterwater
were paid and moved out. The water covered their fields and the river crossing at
the west end of the highway, where the road ran down a shallow wash to the
lake. In 1983 a flash flood cut that wash back two hundred metres in a night
and took the road with it; the highway now ends at a barricade on the lip of
a canyon that wasn't there when it was built. At its other end it leaves the cliffs and goes down a
long wash to a ferry landing on the south shore. Below the Sorrel's mouth the canyon is drowned, so
the red walls go straight down into green water.

For a while the lake brought people. In 1992 the Pruitts' son bulldozed a dirt
bike course in the flat behind the station: a loop with banked turns, rollers,
a double jump and a tabletop, and riders came out on weekends.

## Drought

For twenty years the lake has been falling. It has left a white ring of
mineral crust on the cliffs above the water, the bathtub ring. The stone
chapel of Bitterwater stands at the water's edge again, on cracked mud where
the Sorrel's drowned mouth has drawn back. The ferry stopped
four years ago because the ramp no longer reached the water. The station closed
two years ago. The pumps are still there.

A fire in 1998 burned a long strip of the plateau forest west of the cinder
cone. It is grassland now, gold in summer, with grey snags standing in
it and aspen coming back along the edges.

## What lives here

Ponderosa pine on the plateau, with aspen in the damp hollows and grass
meadows between. Pinyon and juniper on the benches between the cliffs.
Sagebrush, rabbitbrush and scattered juniper on the desert, cottonwood and
willow along the river, tamarisk along the lake shore. Mule deer on the
plateau, pronghorn on the open flats, bighorn sheep on the cliffs, coyotes,
jackrabbits, ravens and vultures over everything, lizards on the warm rock.
