# Lowmouth

The Marrow is a big brown river, and for six thousand years it has been building
this country out of its own mud. Every flood dropped a skin of silt over the
banks, the banks stood a little higher than the swamp behind them, and the
river ran on top of its own levees toward the Gulf. The land it made is never
more than a few metres above the sea. Everything here is low, and most of it is
wet.

## The old course

For most of those years the Marrow left Sorrow Lake, a long oxbow in the north,
and ran south-west past the high ground to the sea. Its natural levee was the
only dry land worth the name. People farmed along it, and in the century before
last they planted sugar cane on the high ground behind it and built a sugar
house on the bank. The parish town grew up further down, where the old channel
met the bay: a white board church called St. Anne of the Marrow with a square
tower, a street of houses, and the tombs. The dead are buried above the ground
in whitewashed vaults, because a grave dug in this country fills with water.

Behind the levee lay the back swamp: bald cypress standing in black water on
their great flared feet, tupelo among them, knees coming up out of the mud, moss
hanging from every limb. The river reached it only in floods.

## The flood of the old century

A hundred and ten years ago the Marrow rose higher than anyone had seen. It
broke through its own levee just below Sorrow Lake and found a shorter, steeper
way to the sea, south-east across the flats, and it never went back. The old
channel below the breach dried, then half filled with silt.

The parish is still arguing about what happened that week. The facts are these:
the steamer Belle Marrow, which ran the old channel to the parish landing, went
down in the flood with everyone aboard; the river took the new course; and the
cane on Mire's land, which should have drowned, did not. People say old Mire
went to the crossroads where the levee road meets the parish road on the second
night of the flood and stood there until morning, and that the river turned
away from his cane at dawn. Nobody says what he gave for it.

## The sinking

A delta lobe that the river no longer feeds starts to sink. Silt settles and
squeezes down, and nothing new comes to build it back up. Within a lifetime the
sea had come in over the old lobe. The parish went first: the church floor is a
metre under the bay now, and the houses are sunk to their eaves, their roofs
and chimneys standing out of the water. Where the salt reached the edge of the
back swamp it killed the cypress, and they still stand there, grey and
barkless, a ghost forest where the cormorants dry their wings.

The back swamp sank too, but slowly, and it stayed fresh. It is wider now and
wetter, black water a metre deep among the trees, with knolls of dry ground
here and there. The flats east of the new river let down into a sheet of
sawgrass standing in a hand or two of clear water, with hammocks of palm and oak
standing out of it like islands. Between the two rivers a piece of marsh came
loose from its bed and floats now, a trembling prairie that gives under your
feet, with the same pale marsh lights over it after dark as the bogs of the old
country the first settlers came from.

The crevasses the flood cut through the levees became bayous: Bayou Lune from
the trembling prairie down to the bay, Bayou Grue from the sawgrass to the new
mouth, Bayou Noir from the swamp out to the sound.

## Lowmouth

The people who stayed moved up the old channel to where it leaves the swamp, and
built on posts over the water. That is Lowmouth: board houses and fishing camps
on cypress posts two metres up, in rows along plank boardwalks laid out like
streets. Front Street runs east and west across the village and on over the old
channel to the bank, the Back Walk crosses it north and south, and short walks
branch off both, each house with a gangway to its door. The net sheds stand at
the edges, and houseboats are tied up in the channel. Everyone has a boat. The quickest way to
anywhere is by water.

Up on the levee there is still a shell road from the west fields to the landing
on the new river, where Theriot keeps a bait shop and everyone puts in. The
sugar house stands roofless with its chimney, the kettles rusting inside. The
parish built a pump house on the levee after the flood to drain the cane.

## The new mouth

Where the new course meets the Gulf, the river's silt builds a new lobe. The
water is brackish there, and red mangroves have taken it: low dark trees
standing in the shallows on arching prop roots, the channels winding between
them, black mangroves on the mud keys. Crocodiles haul out on the banks of the
mouth. Their fresh-water cousins, the alligators, keep to the swamp and the
sawgrass. A shrimp boat, the Miss Odile, sank at her mooring there and her
wheelhouse still stands out of the water.

South of everything runs the chenier, an old ridge of oyster shell the waves
piled up long before the river came this way. Live oaks bent by the Gulf wind
grow on it, and palms. After the flood the parish buried its dead there, in a
new row of tombs above the water. A light stands on the chenier's point.

## What people see

Most of what happens here has a plain cause. A few things do not, and they keep
to the old parish and the crossroads, as if the flood left something unfinished
at both.

At the crossroads there is a corner lot that has been empty for as long as
anyone remembers. Between midnight and three in the morning there is a juke
joint on it, lamps lit and the door standing open. In the morning the lot is
empty again. On the same nights someone draws a figure in chalk on the road at
the crossing and leaves candles burning round it, with a bottle and a glass.
After dark, from far up the road, people have seen a tall thin man standing at
the crossing in a long coat and a wide hat, leaning on a cane. He is never there
when they arrive. Along the parish road where it runs through Mire's burned
cane, someone has staked woven lattices of sticks.

On the bay, after dark, the windows of the drowned houses show lamplight under
the water. From far off you can sometimes see the Belle Marrow out on the bay,
lit at every window, with steam up. Close by, there is only the bay.

The old people of Lowmouth put bottles on the dead limbs of trees on the
hammocks, blue and violet glass to catch whatever wanders at night. The oldest,
the grandmother tree, stands on a hammock in the sawgrass. And in the swamp
there is an alligator half as long again as any other, white as a tomb, that
people call Old Pale. It is seen now and then, and never twice in the same
place.
