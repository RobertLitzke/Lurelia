# Windbarrow

Open chalk country under a golden grass, in the fourth millennium before now.
The grass runs to the horizon in every direction and the wind never stops. The
people here are farmers and horse-keepers who bury their dead under mounds and
have raised one great carved temple on a hill. Nothing is ruined; the villages
are lived in, the fires are lit, and everyone is out with the herds.

## The land

The rock is chalk, laid down as lime mud in a warm sea and lifted without being
much folded. It weathers to whaleback hills with dry valleys between
them, and harder cores in the beds have left knolls standing over the general
swell, so from a valley floor the next hill usually shuts off everything beyond
it. There is no coast: the downs go on past every horizon. Within the chalk
are two thin bands of flint, hard black nodules that stand out as a level line
on the face of every scarp high enough to reach them. The flint is why people
came.

The one break in the rolling country is the Barrow Edge, a scarp some
twenty-six metres high where the chalk snapped along a curving line. It comes in
from the north, swings south-east across the middle of the land in bays and
noses, and leaves by the east, so that the whole north-east stands behind it.
Dry combes are cut back into its face wherever water once came over. The high
ground behind it is the Flint Down, the bleakest part of the country: thin turf
over rubble, more flint than soil.

The great herds are gone from the country but not from living memory. Two herds
of aurochs, ten to twenty head apiece, hold the whole land between them, and a
man may go a season without seeing one. Mammoth are fewer still. High over the
downs, perhaps once in a season, something the size of a cart sails past on the
wind, pale as the grass and shaped like the skates but ten times the size.

Water is scarce. One river, the Narrow, rises in springs at the foot of the
Edge, winds west across the middle of the land below the barrow ridge in a
shallow green valley, turns south at Durn, and ends in Saltmere, a long lake
with no outlet in the south-west hollow. Every other valley in the country is
dry: they were cut in wetter ages and run down to the Narrow from all sides. The lake
is a quarter salt; a white crust rings it in summer and the ground round it
grows nothing but low grey scrub. The valley of the Narrow is the only green
line in the country and everything lives along it.

## The grass

The cover is feathergrass: a bunchgrass that throws up a long silver-gold awn
in summer, waist-high on the open downs and shoulder-high in the swales. In
gusts it lies over in sheets, and the sheets travel, so from a hilltop the wind
is visible crossing the land for a mile. Between the tussocks the ground is bare
chalk rubble and thyme. Where the soil is deeper the grass is greener and
thicker; on the Flint Down it is short and sparse, and the chalk shows through.

Trees grow only where the grass cannot hold: along the Narrow (willow, alder,
birch), in small stands of birch and alder in the damp bottoms of the dry
valleys, in the heads of the combes, and as single hawthorns and junipers on
steep chalk the herds do not reach. A hawthorn standing alone on the downs is a
landmark and generally has something tied to it.

Wind is the constant fact of the country. It blows from the west-south-west and
drops only at dawn. The trees along the river lean, the thatch is tied down with
ropes and stones, and the villages all sit in hollows or on the lee side of a
ridge.

One hollow in the northern downs is white. The grass in it is rimed and
glittering at any hour of any season, and the air over it is cold enough to see
your breath on a midsummer afternoon. Nothing else about it is wrong. The trail
north goes round its rim, there is a post on the lip facing in, and the sheep
are not let down.

## The people

They have been here some six hundred years. They keep cattle, sheep and horses,
grow barley in small walled plots, and dig flint out of the Flint Down with
antler picks. There are two villages and a summer camp:

Below Durn, on the rise between the houses and the water, stands one enormous
oak, wider than it is tall, on a trunk four men cannot reach round. It was
there before the village and the village is named for it only in the sense
that nobody has ever thought it needed a name of its own.

**Durn**, the larger, lies in a hollow on the south bank of the Narrow: eleven
round houses of wattle and daub under deep conical straw roofs, a longhouse for
the gathering, granaries raised on posts out of reach of mice, stock pens of
woven hazel, and racks of hides drying in the wind. The paths through it are
worn to bare chalk.

**Crest**, the smaller, stands in the lee of a ridge north of the barrow field,
closer to the flint and further from the water: seven houses and one longhouse.
Its people are the diggers.

**The summer camp** on the Flint Down is four huts, a windbreak and the bone
house beside the open flint pits, used in the dry months only. Nothing grows up
there to build with, so the bone house is built of mammoth: nine skulls set as a
footing ring, ribs arched over them for rafters, hides lashed over the ribs and
two pairs of tusks meeting over the door. The stock fences round it are ribs set
upright in the chalk. The bone is old and comes out of the ground a morning's
walk east.

Between all of these run trails a foot and a half wide, trodden through the
grass and kept open by use. They follow the ridges, not the valleys, because
the ridges are dry and you can see along them.

## The Bone Downs

East and south of the pits the chalk stops giving flint and starts giving bone.
Skulls the size of a cart weather out of the rubble, ribs stand out of the
ground in rows, and splinters of ivory lie about underfoot. The diggers worked
east until they met it and then stopped; there is a post where they stopped. The
bone is mammoth, and far more of it than the country has mammoth now.

## The barrows

The dead go under mounds on the long ridge between Durn and Crest, where the
ground is highest and the grass shortest. There are twenty-two in the field, standing in three loose knots rather than a
line, because a family buries its own together. Most are round kurgans, seven
to fourteen metres across and a few metres high, some ringed with a kerb of
chalk blocks and most with a post of split oak standing on the crown. Five are
much larger, raised over people the whole country turned out for; the largest,
the Mare's Barrow, is thirty-four metres across and eight high, and you can see
it from everywhere on the ridge.

One, the Long Barrow, is not round: a ridge of heaped earth thirty-four metres
long with a kerbed forecourt at its east end and a stone-lined passage running
in. It is the oldest thing built here and it is open — the slab at its mouth
was pushed aside long ago and never put back.

## The turf maze and the thirteenth pillar

Two things stand out in the empty quarters, and neither village claims either.

On the southern downs, a day from anywhere, a spiral is cut through the turf to
the white chalk, eighty-four metres across and three turns deep, with a post at
its mouth. The grass is kept off it. Nobody in Durn will say who walks it.

At the heart of it is a shaft: two metres square, collared in three courses of
chalk block with ochre laid along the top, two uprights and a lintel over the
way in, and a drop of twenty metres into the dark. No flint was ever dug on
these downs. Air comes steadily up out of it, as it does at the Long Barrow,
and in the same weathers.

In the empty north, on the highest crest for a mile, stands a pillar of the same
cream limestone as the Tepe and taller than any of the twelve: seven and a half
metres, with a wind skate cut into its broad south face and ochred. There is no
other stone like it within a day's walk, and the trail that goes to it goes
nowhere else.

## The Tepe

On a hill west of the barrow field stands the temple, and it is the one thing
here that nobody in Durn can explain the building of. It is older than the
villages. A ring of twelve T-shaped limestone pillars, each three to four
metres tall, stands inside a chalk bench wall that comes to the chest, banked
on the outside; two taller pillars stand at the centre, facing out east. Every pillar
is carved in low relief with animals: horses, aurochs, cranes, wolves, and, on
the two central ones, a long-toothed cat and a bear far bigger than any bear.
The stone was quarried somewhere else; there is no limestone like it in the
country. Red ochre is still in the grooves of the carving.

The people keep the enclosure clear of grass and leave things in it, but they
did not raise it.

## The caves

The chalk under the Flint Down is hollow. Water running down the joints has
dissolved a system of passages, dry now, with high roofs and floors of chalk
rubble. It is entered at two
places: a cave mouth in the head of the combe called the Gape, and a second in
the head of the combe south of it. The passages run for hundreds of metres,
link into two large chambers, and four swallow holes drop daylight into them
from the open down.

At night there are lights over the barrow field: cold yellow flames the size of
a fist, a dozen of them, drifting slowly between the mounds a little above the
grass and pulsing as they go. The villages call them the candles and say they
are the breath going by. They keep to the ridge and to the valley below it, and
they are never seen anywhere else.

Cave bears live in them, and are the reason the flint diggers work in the open
pits and not in the caves. They are far larger than the bears of the river
woods — standing, half again the height of a man — and they den deep, coming out
at the mouths at dawn and dusk. Outside each mouth the chalk is trodden black
and broken bone lies in it, and the walls at the entrances are scored by claws
at the height of a man's head.

## Not accounted for

- **The breath of the Long Barrow, and of the shaft in the maze.** Air comes
  steadily out of the open mouth of the Long Barrow, in all weathers, including
  the dawn calm when nothing else moves. The shaft at the heart of the turf maze
  does the same, and the two are forty minutes' walk apart with nothing between
  them. The grass for ten metres round the forecourt is combed flat and away in
  a ring, and stays flat.
- **The green kurgan.** One mound in the barrow field, near the north end, is
  green when the whole field is gold, and has been as long as anyone recalls.
  It was opened twice by people looking for the reason and there is nothing
  under it: no body, no pot, no bone.
- **The cat and the bear on the central pillars.** No one in Durn has seen
  either animal, and the carvings are older than the villages. The same
  long-toothed cat is scratched, roughly and much more recently, on a block of
  chalk at the mouth of the Gape — by someone who went in far enough to see one.

The strangeness is in those three things and nowhere else. The further you walk
from the barrow ridge, the Tepe and the Gape, the more ordinary the country is.

## The White Horse and the dew pond

South of the Narrow, on the flank of the hill that faces the villages across
the valley, a horse is cut through the turf to the chalk: a hundred paces from
nose to tail, at full stretch, its feet downhill so that it is read from below.
The first people of Durn cut it, and it is scoured clean every seventh summer.
Nothing about it is unexplained; it is the one mark on the land the villages
will tell you they made.

Far out in the dry south-east, a morning from the river, a saucer was dug on a
hilltop and puddled with clay and straw. It fills from the rain and the mist
and has never been known to dry, which is what keeps the herds on the far downs
through the summer.

## Points of interest

- The White Horse on its hillside south of the Narrow, and the dew pond beyond.

- The Tepe: the carved pillar enclosure on its hill, seen from the barrow ridge.
- The Mare's Barrow and the three knots of barrows along the ridge.
- The great oak below Durn.
- The Long Barrow, its open mouth and the ring of flattened grass.
- The green kurgan at the north end of the field.
- Durn, on the Narrow, with its longhouse and granaries.
- Crest, in the lee of the ridge, and the flint pits and summer camp above it.
- The Gape, the scratched block at its mouth, and the cave system under the
  Flint Down.
- The Bone Downs, east of the flint pits, and the bone house at the camp.
- The frost hollow in the northern downs.
- The Skate Pillar on the northern crest, and the turf maze in the south.
- Saltmere and its white shore in the south-west.
- The Barrow Edge, its combes, and the flint band along its face.

## Time

Late afternoon in high summer, about half past four: the grass at its most
gold, the gusts running, long shadows off the barrows.
