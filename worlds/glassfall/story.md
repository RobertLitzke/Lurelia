# Glassfall

The island is the top of an old volcano. Its cone stands in the north-west, a
long time quiet: the summit is a shallow crater of scree and heather with a
cold little tarn in its floor, filled by rain and snowmelt, and the
lava that ran from it reached the west coast and stopped there as black cliffs
and columns. The ash it threw out lies under the whole island as a pale band a
metre or so down, wherever anyone has dug.

The ground is older than the volcano in places. An old fault runs south of the cone, and its south side was heaved up into a long rock face through the woods; a shorter scarp crosses the north-east hills. On the cone's eastern flank, lava once crusted over and drained out from beneath, leaving a tube of rock you can walk through. Its roof has fallen in at two places, and the holes drop straight into it.

Long after the mountain went quiet, something fell out of the sky. It came in
low from the south-west and struck the eastern hills, leaving a bowl ninety
metres across with a raised rim, steeper on its north-east side where the
throw went. The floor of the bowl is not rock. It is a sheet of dark glass,
smooth enough to see the sky in, and nothing grows on it. Pieces of the thing
that fell were thrown north-east across the hills in a long oval, the small ones
close to the crater and the heaviest furthest out. The islanders called them
skystone: black glass shot through with metal, faintly warm to hold.

Around the skystone, life grew wrong. Pines near the crater wind upward in a
slow spiral and come out pale. Birches bleach. Some shrubs grow leaves as hard
and clear as the crater floor, and spikes of that glass push up through the turf.
Over each buried fragment the moss is a different green. Hares there are pale
and do not run from people, and big violet moths fly in the open. The
strangeness is strongest at the crater and fades along the strewn field, so that
walking away from it the island becomes slowly ordinary again. Nobody ever
explained it.

Not every fragment came down in the strewn field. Three of the largest flew
wide, one north toward the coast, one south-east past where the village would
stand, and one into the southern woods below the fault scarp. Each buried
itself, and round each the bloom took hold just as it had at the crater, in a
pale glade about the size of the tarn by the scarp, with ordinary forest all
round it.

About a hundred and fifty years ago settlers came for the skystone. They landed
on the east shore and built a plank landing stage there, and a cart track up
the valley. Their village stood on the flat ground beside the river that drains
the volcano's south-east flank: stone cottages with thatched roofs, a longhouse
with a slate roof, a well, a little chapel with a bell-cote, and a mill whose
wheel turned in the river. They dug skystone out of the crater rim and kept
their tools in a shed beside the workings. They walled small fields.

The settlers found the three outlying glades by their pale trees and went
after the stones in them. At the north glade they put up a second shed to sort
and store what they dug. At the south glade the fragment had gone into the
scarp, so they drove a short timbered adit after it and left a cart at its
mouth. One of the collectors built himself a plank hut in the south-east glade
and lived there for a summer. All three stood empty when the village did.

They had a surveyor. He mapped the island and marked every fragment he found,
because the settlers believed the fragments all pointed back toward one large
mass, which they called the mother stone. He set up his last camp on the high
ground north of the volcano, and his instrument is still there on its tripod,
levelled and aimed out to sea at nothing anyone can see. He was not seen again.

Ninety years ago a storm flood broke the river out of its bed just above the
village. The water cut a new course east toward the sea, and in a season the
old channel past the houses was a line of dry cobbles. The flood left a
layer of silt across the village, half burying the lower walls. Without the
river the mill stood dry and the fields failed, and within two years the
settlers left. The village has been falling down ever since: roofs first,
then the timber, then the upper courses of stone. Moss has the rest.

Forty years ago a fire started on the western slopes and the wind carried it
north-east. It took the pine forest on that side of the volcano. The slope is
prairie now, gold grass with grey snags standing in it, aspen and young pines
coming back along the edges where the fire stopped.

Out on that prairie stands a stone doorway, on its own, with nothing behind
it. Its sill is worn hollow, as if people walked through it for years. It is
older than the village. Nobody in the village built it.
