# Hemlow

A stone village on a knoll above a small river, on a midsummer evening. The
light is low and gold, the fields are full of sheep and the hay is full of
flowers, and the wood past the last wall is not an ordinary wood.

## The land

The country is limestone: a warm, honey-coloured oolite laid down as lime sand
in a shallow tropical sea, then lifted and tilted so that it ends in the east
in a long curving scarp, Hemlow Edge, some twenty metres high. Below the Edge a
narrow vale runs down to the sea, which is the only edge the country has: west,
north and south it goes on, wood behind wood, past every horizon.

Above the Edge the land is a rolling upland, never level for long. It was cut
into shallow valleys in wetter ages, before the land broke, and they are dry
now; where they reach the Edge they hang in its face as combes. The highest
ground is in the north and along a ridge in the south.

One valley still holds water. The Hem rises in Hemmere, a shallow lake in a
hollow at the north-west of the upland, fed by springs that come up through the
peat round its shore. It leaves the mere by the south-east, bends south round
the village fields, runs east under the foot of Hemlow Knap, and winds on in
long loops to the Edge. There it goes over in one drop, Lantern Spout, into a
pool at the foot of the cliff, and wanders across the vale to the sea.

Round the mere the springs keep the ground wet all year. The wet ground is the
Wisp Moss: sphagnum, peat pools, reedmace, willow scrub and dead alders standing
in the water. Nobody cuts peat there now.

Hemlow Knap is a broad rounded hill of harder stone standing a few metres over
the valley floor, its south foot in the river.

## The village and its fields

Hemlow was a wool village. Its people quarried the oolite from the face of the
Edge and built everything of it: houses, walls, barn, church. In the 1300s,
when the fleeces of the upland sold to Flanders, the village rebuilt its church
on the top of the Knap with a square tower that can be seen from the whole
valley, and the abbey that owned the land built a great tithe barn below it.
The mill on the Hem, at the foot of the Knap, is older than both.

The village is thirteen buildings round a small green on the top of the Knap
and down a cobbled lane to the river: the church, the inn (the Hob and
Lantern), the tithe barn, the mill, a dovecote behind the church, a row of
three weavers' cottages, two larger houses and five cottages, roofed in stone
slates or thatch. A packhorse bridge of three low arches crosses the Hem below
the mill, where the old road went south. A market cross stands on the green.

The same wool paid for the walls. Round the Knap the land is parcelled into
fields behind dry-stone walls, each with its name: Church Leaze and Stone Leaze
north of the village, with the track to the wood running between them; West
Field down toward the river; Ford Ground and Bridge Close south of the bridge,
either side of the old road. These are pasture, grazed short, and the flock is
out in them. Barn Ground to the east and Mere Close to the south-west are
barley. What is not walled is hay meadow, thick with buttercup, scabious,
knapweed and ox-eye daisy. There are hurdle folds in Stone Leaze and Ford
Ground, and one farm outside the village, Ford Farm, east of the old road, with
its byre, its well, its fold and its bees.

Nobody is about. It is Midsummer Eve, and the village has gone down to the
river meadows for the evening as it does every year; you arrive after they have
gone and before they come back. By custom a lamp is left burning in every
window, so the Hollins can see the way in and out of the village.

## The Hollins

All round the fields lies the wood the village calls the Hollins. It has always
been thought to belong to someone else. The village keeps its walls between
the fields and the trees and does not take timber from it.

It is not one wood. On the high ground it is beech: tall grey trunks close
together over a floor of brown leaves where almost nothing grows. In the wet
hollows it is alder, willow and birch over fern to the waist, every trunk
green, with mist lying in it by evening. Between them it is oak and hazel. Here
and there is a glade, a ring of grass and flowers that the trees stop at, with
nothing anyone can point to that keeps them out.

And in two places it is plainly strange. North of the Knap, round the Hob
Stones, and in the Old Hollins in the south-west, the oaks stand a hundred
feet high and as far round as a cottage, with gold maples and wisteria grown
into trees between them, blossom hanging from every bough, pale birches, clumps
of glassy violet glassbloom, deer with moss on their backs and faintly lit
antlers, rays that swim through the air between the trunks, and now and then
among the deer a white one with a single horn. The strangeness fades with
distance: in the far parts of the Hollins it shows only as some gold in the
leaves, and in the meadows as the glow of the moths and the fireflies after
sunset.

The Hob Stones are a ring of weathered standing stones in a clearing in the
north wood. They were old when the first houses were built. A row of smaller
stones, the Grey Wethers, leads toward them from the end of the track between
the leazes.

In the Old Hollins there is the Moon Pool, still water that no stream runs
into or out of, and beyond it a round green mound that nobody raised, with a
stone doorway standing on it and nothing behind the doorway. Its sill is worn
hollow. That is Hob's Door. A path nobody admits to keeping runs to both from
the charcoal burners' clamp.

## Lantern Spout, Lantern Hole and the Dell

Beside the fall at Lantern Spout, at the level of the vale, a cave mouth opens
in the face of the Edge. Behind it is Lantern Hole: not one passage but a maze
of them, all on a level, running back under the wood in loops, meeting in
chambers and coming out again through two more mouths further along the Edge.
The stone is the same honey limestone as the village. The roofs of the
chambers are covered in glow-worms, thousands of blue-green points, enough to
walk by: the Lantern Room in the north, the Candle Room further in, and Hob's
Larder, the deepest. Each chamber has a shaft up into the wood.

In the middle of the maze the roof of the biggest cavern fell in long ago and
left Lantern Dell, a round pit open to the sky, its walls hung with fern and
vine, its floor green with moss that glows faintly, with a pool where the drips
collect. Three passages open into it. A ravine, Hob's Gill, comes down into it
from the wood, and is the one way in that needs no light: its head is a short
walk from the wise woman's cottage.

The face of the Edge is hung with a beech hanger, ferns and vines over the lip.

## Unexplained

- The lights over the Wisp Moss. Pale blue-white lights drift low over the
  peat pools after sunset, singly and in twos, and the same lights are seen in
  the wet hollows of the wood. Fireflies are green-gold and these are not.
- The glow-worms of Lantern Hole grow thickest in the chambers nearest the Hob
  Stones.
- The ring of the Hob Stones. Grass grows in the clearing but no tree, and the
  glassbloom grows in a ring round it.
- The glades. Nothing is cut and nothing grazes them, and the trees do not
  come in.
- Hob's Door. The sill is worn by feet, and nobody in the village will say they
  have walked through it.

People who carry a hag stone, a pebble with a hole worn through it, say they
see the Hollins as they are. They turn up along the Hem, in the glades, in the
wet hollows and on the floor of the Dell.

## Points of interest

- The church tower on the top of the Knap, seen from everywhere in the valley.
- The green, the market cross, the inn.
- The mill and the packhorse bridge at the foot of the Knap.
- The walled fields, the folds, and Ford Farm.
- The Hob Stones and the Grey Wethers in the north wood.
- The Moon Pool and Hob's Door in the Old Hollins.
- Lantern Spout, the cave mouth beside it, Lantern Hole and its three chambers.
- Lantern Dell and Hob's Gill.
- Hemmere and the Wisp Moss in the north-west.

## Time

The world opens at about six in the evening on Midsummer Eve: low gold light
from the west, long shadows across the fields, and the fireflies starting in
the long grass.
