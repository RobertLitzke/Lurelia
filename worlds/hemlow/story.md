# Hemlow

A stone village on a knoll above a small river, on a midsummer evening. The
light is low and gold, the fields are full of sheep and the hay is full of
flowers, and the wood past the last wall is not an ordinary wood. North of it
is the market town where its wool was sold, west of it a faerie down and a
ruined abbey, south of it the redwoods, and east of it a cliff with something
older on top.

## The land

The country is limestone: a warm, honey-coloured oolite laid down as lime sand
in a shallow tropical sea, then lifted and tilted so that it breaks off across
the south in a long curving scarp, Hemlow Edge, some twenty metres high. Below
the Edge a vale runs down through the redwoods toward the coast and the whaling
town of Gannet Harbor, a day's walk south. In the west the Edge dies away among
the redwoods.

Above the Edge the land is a rolling upland, never level for long. It was cut
into shallow valleys in wetter ages, before the land broke, and they are dry
now; where they reach the Edge they hang in its face as combes.

Along the east the upland stops against Raven Scar, a sheer wall of stone forty
metres high where an older block of country stands over the oolite along a
fault. Ravens nest on its ledges. In the north-east the old block comes down to
the oolite in a long slope instead, the rising ground, and the north road runs
up it.

Two rivers. The Wend comes off the high ground in the east, goes over Raven
Scar in one white drop, Wend Force, into a pool at its foot, and winds west
through the Hollins into Hemmere, a shallow lake in a hollow north-west of the
village, which springs also feed through the peat round its shore. The Hem
leaves the mere by the south-east, bends east round the village fields, runs
south past the foot of Hemlow Knap, and winds on in long loops to the Edge.
There it goes over at Lantern Spout into a pool at the foot of the cliff, and
runs on down the vale, wider now, through the redwoods to become Gannet's Cold
River.

In the west the Fleece Brook rises among the farms and runs south down a
shallow valley past the abbey to the redwoods and the coast.

Round the mere the springs keep the ground wet all year. The wet ground is the
Wisp Moss: sphagnum, peat pools, reedmace, willow scrub and dead alders standing
in the water. Nobody cuts peat there now.

Hemlow Knap is a broad rounded hill of harder stone standing a few metres over
the valley floor, its east foot in the river.

## The village and its fields

Hemlow was a wool village. Its people quarried the oolite from the face of the
Edge and built everything of it: houses, walls, barn, church. In the 1300s,
when the fleeces of the upland sold to Flanders, the village rebuilt its church
on the top of the Knap with a square tower that can be seen from the whole
valley, and the abbey that owned the land built a great tithe barn below it.
The mill on the Hem, at the foot of the Knap, is older than both.

The village is two dozen buildings on the top of the Knap, where the lane
from the west meets the lane down from the north and the two go on together
down to the river: the church, the inn (the Hob and Lantern), the tithe barn,
the smithy and the bakehouse, the almshouses, a dovecote, a cart shed, and
stone cottages, rows and larger houses along the three lanes, roofed in stone
slates or thatch. The mill stands on the Hem at the foot of the Knap. A
packhorse bridge of three low arches crosses below it, where the old road
went east. A market cross stands on the green where the lanes meet.

The same wool paid for the walls. Round the Knap the land is parcelled into
fields behind dry-stone walls, each with its name: Church Leaze and Stone Leaze
west of the village, with the track to the wood running between them; West
Field down toward the river; Ford Ground and Bridge Close east of the bridge,
either side of the old road. These are pasture, grazed short, and the flock is
out in them. Barn Ground to the south and Mere Close to the north-east are
barley. What is not walled is hay meadow, thick with buttercup, scabious,
knapweed and ox-eye daisy. There are hurdle folds in Stone Leaze and Ford
Ground, and one farm outside the village, Ford Farm, south of the old road, with
its byre, its well, its fold and its bees.

Nobody is about. It is Midsummer Eve, and the village has gone down to the
river meadows for the evening as it does every year; you arrive after they have
gone and before they come back. By custom a lamp is left burning in every
window, so the Hollins can see the way in and out of the village.

## Chepworth and the farms

Chepworth is the market town on the north road, where Hemlow and every village
round sold its wool. It was rich in the 1400s and built in stone: a long high
street that swells into a market place, a market hall on open arches with the
wool room over it, a church with a tower to answer Hemlow's, a coaching inn,
the wool merchants' houses and rows along the lanes off it either side,
almshouses, and a great barn at the edge of the town. It is as empty as Hemlow.

West of the town are the farms, walled fields of sheep, barley and hay with an
orchard among them: Wethercote, with its dew pond, and Holloway. Below them the
Fleece Brook was dammed into a stone-sided pool, the sheepwash, where the flocks
were washed before shearing, with pens either side and the abbey's shearing
barn beside it. A drove road, worn down between its banks, runs south from the
farms past the sheepwash and the abbey to the redwoods.

## The abbey

Down the brook's valley stands the ruin of Hemlow Abbey, which owned the land
and built the tithe barn. It was dissolved in 1539 and robbed for stone, and
Chepworth's best houses have carved fragments in their walls. What is left is a
roofless church with its east gable and empty window standing, broken arcades,
the footings of the cloister in the grass, the monks' graveyard and a row of
fishponds stepped down the brook. Its stone came from Abbey Quarry to the
south, a deep pit in the oolite with a green pool in its floor and the
stone-cutters' lodge at its lip.

## The Pucklands

South of Chepworth the common grazing of Chepworth Down runs open and unwalled
toward the Hollins, and as it goes it turns strange. The turf goes very short
and very green. Rings of toadstools stand in it, some tens of metres across,
and the sheep will not graze inside them. At the heart of it is Puck's Barrow,
a long mound of turf and stone older than anything else in the country, its
open end to the east and the stone that closed it pushed aside. Glassbloom
grows round its forecourt. Pale hares feed on the down at dusk, and after dark
the fae horses come out of the Hollins to graze it. The village calls the whole
of it the Pucklands and goes round.

## The Hollins

All round the fields lies the wood the village calls the Hollins. It has always
been thought to belong to someone else. The village keeps its walls between
the fields and the trees and does not take timber from it.

It is not one wood. On the high ground it is beech: tall grey trunks close
together over a floor of brown leaves where almost nothing grows. In the wet
hollows it is alder, willow and birch over fern to the waist, every trunk
green, with mist lying in it by evening. Between them it is oak and hazel. Here
and there is a glade, a ring of grass and flowers that the trees stop at, with
nothing anyone can point to that keeps them out. In the south it thins into the
redwoods.

And in places it is plainly strange. West of the Knap, round the Hob Stones,
and in the Old Hollins in the east under Raven Scar, the oaks stand a hundred
feet high and as far round as a cottage, with gold maples and wisteria grown
into trees between them, blossom hanging from every bough, pale birches, clumps
of glassy violet glassbloom, deer with moss on their backs and faintly lit
antlers, rays that swim through the air between the trunks, and now and then
among the deer a white one with a single horn. The strangeness fades with
distance: in the far parts of the Hollins it shows only as some gold in the
leaves, and in the meadows as the glow of the moths and the fireflies after
sunset.

The Hob Stones are a ring of weathered standing stones in a clearing in the
west wood. They were old when the first houses were built. A row of smaller
stones, the Grey Wethers, leads toward them from the end of the track between
the leazes, and a footpath runs on from them over the Pucklands to Chepworth.

In the Old Hollins there is the Moon Pool, still water that no stream runs
into or out of, and beyond it a round green mound that nobody raised, with a
stone doorway standing on it and nothing behind the doorway. Its sill is worn
hollow. That is Hob's Door. A path nobody admits to keeping runs to both from
the charcoal burners' clamp.

## Lantern Spout, Lantern Hole and the Dell

Beside the fall at Lantern Spout, at the level of the vale, a cave mouth opens
in the face of the Edge. Behind it is Lantern Hole: not one passage but a maze
of them, all on a level, running back under the wood in loops, meeting in
chambers and coming out again through two more mouths further along the Edge.
The stone is the same honey limestone as the village. The roofs of the
chambers are covered in glow-worms, thousands of blue-green points, enough to
walk by: the Lantern Room in the east, the Candle Room further in, and Hob's
Larder, the deepest. Each chamber has a shaft up into the wood.

In the middle of the maze the roof of the biggest cavern fell in long ago and
left Lantern Dell, a round pit open to the sky, its walls hung with fern and
vine, its floor green with moss that glows faintly, with a pool where the drips
collect. Three passages open into it. A ravine, Hob's Gill, comes down into it
from the wood, and is the one way in that needs no light: its head is a short
walk from the wise woman's cottage.

The face of the Edge is hung with a beech hanger, ferns and vines over the lip.

## The redwoods and Loftwood

Below the Edge the wood changes. Coast redwoods come north out of Gannet's
country, and in the low ground of the south-west, where the sea fog comes up
into them, they grow into giants. In the 1870s Gannet's loggers came north up
the Cold River for timber, reached the oldest grove, and built in it instead of
cutting it: Loftwood, ring platforms round the trunks high over the ground,
walkways strung between them and stairs wound up a few of the trees. Their
first camp is still at the foot of the grove, with the logs from the days they
did cut.

## Raven Scar and the Grimwold

On top of Raven Scar is a forest that belongs to an older world than the
Hollins: monkey-puzzles and ginkgos over tree ferns and cycads, horsetails
taller than a man on the wet ground by the Wend, moss on everything, and a
green dim light. The village calls it the Grimwold and keeps away from the top
of the Scar. The one way up is the switchback, a track cut in terraces up the
great fall of scree under the Scar; from its top a track runs north through
the edge of the Grimwold, over the Wend above the Force and down the rising
ground to the north road. Nobody from the village uses it. They say that what
moves in the Grimwold at dusk is bigger than any deer.

## Unexplained

- The lights over the Wisp Moss. Pale blue-white lights drift low over the
  peat pools after sunset, singly and in twos, and the same lights are seen in
  the wet hollows of the wood and drifting up into the Grimwold. Fireflies are
  green-gold and these are not.
- The glow-worms of Lantern Hole grow thickest in the chambers nearest the Hob
  Stones.
- The ring of the Hob Stones. Grass grows in the clearing but no tree, and the
  glassbloom grows in a ring round it.
- The glades. Nothing is cut and nothing grazes them, and the trees do not
  come in.
- Hob's Door. The sill is worn by feet, and nobody in the village will say they
  have walked through it.
- The rings on the Pucklands, and why the sheep will not go inside them.

People who carry a hag stone, a pebble with a hole worn through it, say they
see the Hollins as they are. They turn up along the Hem, in the glades, in the
wet hollows, on the Pucklands and on the floor of the Dell.

## Points of interest

- The church tower on the top of the Knap, seen from everywhere in the valley.
- The green, the market cross, the inn.
- The mill and the packhorse bridge at the foot of the Knap.
- The walled fields, the folds, and Ford Farm.
- The Hob Stones and the Grey Wethers in the west wood.
- The Moon Pool and Hob's Door in the Old Hollins.
- Lantern Spout, the cave mouth beside it, Lantern Hole and its three chambers.
- Lantern Dell and Hob's Gill.
- Hemmere and the Wisp Moss north-west of the village.
- Chepworth: the market place, the market hall, the church.
- Wethercote and Holloway farms, the sheepwash and the shearing barn.
- The Pucklands and Puck's Barrow.
- Hemlow Abbey and its fishponds, and Abbey Quarry.
- Loftwood in the redwoods.
- Raven Scar, Wend Force, the switchback and the Grimwold.

## Time

The world opens at about six in the evening on Midsummer Eve: low gold light
from the west, long shadows across the fields, and the fireflies starting in
the long grass.
