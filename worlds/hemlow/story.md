# Hemlow

A stone village on a knoll above a small river, on a midsummer evening. The
light is low and gold, the meadows are full of flowers, and the wood that rings
the fields is not quite an ordinary wood.

## The land

The country is limestone: a warm, honey-coloured oolite laid down as lime sand
in a shallow tropical sea, then lifted and tilted so that it ends in the east
in a long scarp, Hemlow Edge, some twenty-five metres high. Below the Edge a narrow
vale runs down to the sea. Above it the land is a gentle upland, folded into
low rises and shallow valleys.

One of those valleys holds the Hem. It rises in Hemmere, a shallow lake in a
hollow at the west end of the upland, fed by springs that come up through the
peat round its shore. The Hem leaves the mere eastward, slow and clear, and
winds through a modest valley across the whole upland. Where the valley meets
the Edge the river goes over it in one drop, Lantern Spout, into a pool at
the foot of the cliff, and then wanders across the vale to the sea.

Round the mere the springs keep the ground wet all year. The wet ground is the
Wisp Moss: sphagnum, peat pools, reedmace, willow scrub and dead alders standing
in the water. Nobody cuts peat there now.

Halfway along the valley the river bends round a knoll of harder stone, Hemlow
Knap. The Knap is a broad rounded hill standing a few metres over the valley
floor, its south foot in the river. Its sides were cleared for sheep long ago
and are now hay meadow and pasture, thick with flowers: buttercup, scabious,
knapweed, ox-eye daisy, poppies in the disturbed ground.

## The village

Hemlow was a wool village. Its people quarried the oolite from the face of the
Edge and built everything of it: houses, walls, barn, church. In the 1300s,
when the fleeces of the upland sold to Flanders, the village rebuilt its church
on the top of the Knap with a square tower that can be seen from the whole
valley, and the abbey that owned the land built a great tithe barn below it.
The mill on the Hem, at the foot of the Knap, is older than both.

The village is thirteen buildings round a small green on the top of the Knap
and down a cobbled lane to the river: the church, the inn (the Hob and
Lantern), the tithe barn, the mill, a dovecote behind the church, a row of
three weavers' cottages, two larger houses and five cottages, roofed in stone
slates or thatch. A packhorse bridge of three low arches crosses the Hem below
the mill, where the old road went south. A market cross stands on the green.
Dry-stone walls run out from the village across the meadows.

Nobody is about. It is Midsummer Eve, and the village has gone down to the
river meadows for the evening as it does every year; you arrive after they have
gone and before they come back. By custom a lamp is left burning in every
window, so the Hollins can see the way in and out of the village.

## The Hollins

All round the fields lies the wood the village calls the Hollins. It is old
oak and beech wood with hazel beneath, and it has always been thought to belong
to someone else. The village keeps its walls between the fields and the trees
and does not take timber from it.

The source of whatever lives in the Hollins is the Hob Stones, a ring of
weathered standing stones in a clearing in the wood north of the Knap. They
were old when the first houses were built. Close to the stones the wood is
plainly strange: maples that keep their gold leaves all summer, wisteria grown
into trees, blossom vines hanging from every bough, pale birches, clumps of
glassy violet glassbloom, deer with moss on their backs and faintly lit
antlers, and rays that swim through the air between the trunks. The strangeness
fades with distance from the stones: in the far parts of the Hollins it is an
ordinary summer wood with some gold in it, and in the meadows it shows only as
the glow of the moths and the fireflies after sunset.

The Hollins come down to the river and the mere on every side, and the far
side of the mere is wholly theirs.

## Lantern Spout and Lantern Hole

Behind the fall at Lantern Spout the river has dissolved a cave back into the
Edge. You can walk in behind the curtain of water. The passage runs back under
the wood and its ceiling is covered in glow-worms, thousands of blue-green
points of light. A shaft comes up from the cave into the wood above.

The face of the Edge is hung with a beech hanger, ferns and vines over the lip.

## Unexplained

- The lights over the Wisp Moss. Pale blue-white lights drift low over the
  peat pools after sunset, singly and in twos. Fireflies are green-gold and
  these are not.
- The glow-worms of Lantern Hole grow thickest in the part of the cave nearest
  the Hob Stones, though the stones are a long way off.
- The ring of the Hob Stones. Grass grows in the clearing but no tree, and the
  glassbloom grows in a ring round it and nowhere else in the clearing.

People who carry a hag stone, a pebble with a hole worn through it, say they
see the Hollins as they are. They turn up along the Hem and round the stones.

## Points of interest

- The church tower on the top of the Knap, seen from everywhere in the valley.
- The green, the market cross, the inn.
- The mill and the packhorse bridge at the foot of the Knap.
- The Hob Stones in their clearing in the north wood.
- Lantern Spout, the plunge pool, and Lantern Hole behind the fall.
- Hemmere and the Wisp Moss at the west end of the valley.

## Time

The world opens at about six in the evening on Midsummer Eve: low gold light
from the west, long shadows across the meadows, and the fireflies starting in
the long grass.
