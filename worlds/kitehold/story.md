# Kitehold

South of Saurischia the old upland comes down to a river, and the river
splits the country in two. West of it is open grass as far as the eye goes,
with one black rock standing up out of it and a town on top of the rock,
flying kites. East of it the land climbs: maple woods along the water, then
redwoods on a bench, then a cliff of cloud forest, and over the cloud a high
wet moor where the plants look like nothing else in the world.

## The land

The Lessa leaves Cascade Mere in Saurischia and comes in over the north edge
already a river. Here it runs south down the foot of the Steps, the fault
that crosses Saurischia from the north-east. The Steps swing south through
Kitehold as two broken faces, lower than in the north and further apart, and
the high country behind them rises higher than anywhere in Saurischia. In
the south the faces die out and the land comes down in low foothills to a
delta: below the farms the Lessa splits among reed marsh, pools and hummocks
into slow channels that wander out over the south edge to the sea.

Cinderhorn stands just over the north edge. Its last eruption, three hundred
years ago, ran south, but long before that, in the first thousand years of
the cone, its lava ran much further: down the old valley in the north-west
of Kitehold and all the way east to the Lessa, which it pushed against the
foot of the Steps. The river cut back down through it and has long since worn
its gorge back into a broad green valley, meadow on the floor and maples on
the slopes, which the holloway follows north to Saurischia.

The old flows are hollow. Lava crusts over as it runs and keeps running
inside the crust, and when the eruption stops the tube drains and stays.
There are three under the north-west, the Skylights, with holes fallen in
their roofs where the light comes down in shafts. Ferns grow in each shaft
and nowhere else. Warm water rises in the floor of the largest, from the
heat that is still down there, and stands in steaming pools. Rain sinks
through the lava and comes out where the flow ends at the west edge as a
cold clear spring, the head of the stream Gannet Harbor calls the east
branch of the Cold River.

On the south edge of the flow the lava thins out over the grass in tongues.
Here and there it went round a knoll and left it standing, an island of old
ground and old trees in black rock; the people call these kipukas.

South of the lava is the Plain, two thousand hectares of grass on the ash
and loess the wind laid down off the flows. Old stream beds lie under it,
buried by ash, and the water still follows them: each shows as a winding
band of one flower through the tan grass, lupine on some, paintbrush or
poppies on others. Twice the water comes near enough the top to stand: a
muddy pan west of the Kite and a smaller one toward the sea, trodden out
and wallowed deeper by the herds, and the only water on the Plain away from
the Lessa. Prairie dogs have three towns on it, one under the lava's edge,
one in the west and one out below the Kite, each a patch of bitten-down
grass with sixty or so burrows.

In the middle of the Plain stands the Kite, a plug of basalt twenty metres
high, the throat of a volcano much older than Cinderhorn whose cone has
long since weathered away. Its sides are columns, broken back in
ledges and bays and fallen into long slopes of rubble round the foot. Its top
is nearly flat, and the town of Kitehold is built on it.

## The wind

The wind is the reason for everything here. It comes in off the sea past
Gannet Harbor, crosses the Plain with nothing to stop it, and hits the
Steps. Forced up the faces it cools, and the cloud forms on the second face
nearly every afternoon and stays most nights. Below the cloud is the wet
of the redwoods; inside it the cloud forest, tree ferns and podocarps
dripping with moss; above it the páramo, the high moor, in fog half the
year and in hard sun the other half.

Streams come off the páramo, which holds water like a sponge, and go over
both faces in falls on their way to the Lessa. The largest comes down beside
the crossing.

## Kitehold

People came onto the Plain with horses, following the wild herds, and found
the Kite the one place a camp could be defended and the one place where the
wind never failed. They built on top and grew grain on the bottomland along
the Lessa, and the wind ground it. The town is low and spreads over the
whole top of the rock, round a circular plaza: houses of stone below and
timber above, canvas awnings dyed in the colours the kite makers use, and
tall timber wind towers that catch the wind over the roofs and push it down
through the rooms. Two windmills grind for the farms.

Kitehold believes that the wind carries what is written on cloth to wherever
it is going. So there are flags everywhere: strings of them across every
street, from every tower to the next, from the stupas on the rim to the
plaza mast. Kites are flown from the rim every day there is wind to fly
them, for the dead, for the harvest, and for no reason at all.

The road up is cut in the side of the rock and climbs in two turns.

## The rest of the country

The road west to Gannet Harbor runs out across the Plain to the Wayfinders'
station, a ring of low stone shelters, a lookout and a lantern beacon on the
edge of the western woods. The Wayfinders keep the road and the beacon. They
still light it, though no one has come from Gannet Harbor in years.

The road up to the town is cut back into the south face and climbs
inside the east rim in a cutting between rock banks.

The pilgrim road goes north from Kitehold to the Skylights. It is the oldest
road in the country, worn three metres down into the Plain by hooves and
carts, so that walking it you see nothing but its banks, and climbing out of
it you see everything. Cairns stand along it. At the skylights there are
shrines, and over the largest a bathhouse cut into the basalt.

Pilgrims drop bell charms and coins down the bathhouse skylight, and they
lie on the tube floor under it round a stone lantern the keepers set there.
Between the Long Tube's first two skylights the roof is lit green by moss
and hung with lava drips, and pale mushrooms grow under it. The West Tube
holds the bones and teeth of cave bears that wintered in it, and deep in the
East Tube, past where the daylight reaches, two old lanterns still glow over
the shards of the people who lived there before Kitehold was built.

The east road leaves Kitehold for the Lessa and crosses at Maplebridge, a
hamlet of maple timber and river cobbles round a covered toll bridge, with a
mill on the tributary that falls in beside it and sugar houses back in the
maples. From Maplebridge the road climbs the Steps in switchbacks, through
the redwoods, and passes through the Hollow Titans, a ring of redwoods grown
together at the root so that the road goes under them. It ends on the
páramo at a stone weather observatory, among cairns hung with cloth.

North from Maplebridge the old road to Saurischia climbs out of the maples
onto the bench above the gorge and runs along it as a holloway, worn three
metres down between earth banks by carts and sap barrels. Cottages stand
along it on the uphill side, their gardens between them and the lane and
steps worn up the bank to each gate. On the gorge side a field wall runs
along the top of the bank, with gaps where people climb up to look over the
river.

The farms lie along the Lessa south of Maplebridge, on the river's west side:
grain, flax, sunflowers, apples. Below them the river slows into a marsh,
and below that the delta.

## The animals

Wild horses run on the Plain, the dun little horses of the steppe and a
larger bay kind, and bison graze along the lava. Small feathered runners
(compsognathus, come down from Saurischia) follow the herds for what they
kick up. Golden eagles hunt the prairie dogs; starlings gather over the farms
at dusk in flocks of hundreds.

Otters, herons and salmon are in the Lessa; deer and foxes in the maples.
In the cloud forest live quetzals, morpho butterflies, and small frogs
coloured like warnings. Vicuñas graze the páramo, hummingbirds with bills
longer than their bodies feed at the frailejones, condors ride the updraft
along the faces, and a black bear with pale rings round its eyes comes up
out of the cloud forest to dig.

## What no one has explained

The Skylights have bats, and they have something else. Out of the
largest skylight at dusk, with the bats, come small winged animals the
size of a dog that are warm to stand near and faintly glow, like the
inside of Cinderhorn's channels. Kitehold calls them ember drakes. They
are not seen anywhere far from the lava, and the further the flow is from
the cone, the fewer there are.

On the climb through the cloud forest there are small wooden lanterns on
posts along the road, carved, old, and lit. Nobody in Kitehold or
Maplebridge says they light them.
